🧩 Mission 1 — Crack the Physiology Code

Example question: Arrange these steps in the correct order: Bone adaptation · Osteocyte mechanosensing · Mechanical loading · Bone-cell signalling.

Mechanical loading→Osteocyte mechanosensing→Bone-cell signalling→Bone adaptation

Example answer: Exercise places mechanical strain on bone. Osteocytes sense this strain and regulate signalling to bone-forming and bone-resorbing cells. With appropriate loading, nutrition and recovery, the skeleton can adapt over time. Why: Bone is a living tissue that responds to mechanical stimuli.

How to answer Q2: Identify the incorrect physiological claim, rewrite it accurately and explain the mechanism in 1–2 sentences.

🔗 Mission 2 — Match the Mechanism

Example question: Match each physiological term to its function.

Physiological featureCorrect function
Stroke volumeBlood ejected per heartbeat
Growth plateLongitudinal bone growth
Oxidative phosphorylationAerobic ATP production
Motor coordinationRefinement of movement skills

Example explanation: Stroke volume contributes to cardiac output (heart rate × stroke volume). Because children generally have smaller absolute stroke volumes than adults, their heart-rate response at a given absolute workload may differ.

How to answer Q3–Q5: Write the matching pairs clearly (for example A–2, B–3), then add brief physiological justification when asked.

🔬 Mission 3 — Explain the WHY

Example question: Why can a child improve strength without large increases in muscle size?

Example answer: Early strength gains can occur through improved motor-unit recruitment, coordination and movement technique. These neural adaptations improve force production without requiring substantial muscle hypertrophy. Exercise link: Skill-based, supervised resistance exercise can develop strength and movement competence.

How to answer Q6–Q8: Use the sequence physiological feature → mechanism → exercise consequence. Avoid answers such as “because children are smaller” without explaining how body size changes the response.

🩺 Mission 4 — Solve the Clinical Case

Example case: A 10-year-old girl becomes tired during repeated shuttle runs but enjoys moderate cycling.

Physiology: Repeated shuttle runs involve acceleration, deceleration and directional changes, which can increase relative metabolic and neuromuscular demands compared with comfortable cycling.

Assess: Ask about recent activity, pace, symptoms, rest intervals and environment. Do not diagnose the cause of fatigue from the scenario alone.

Exercise consideration: Start with tolerable aerobic activities and progressively introduce short shuttle-running drills with adequate recovery and technique coaching.

Why: Exercise should reflect the child's current fitness, maturation, skill and symptom response.

How to answer Q9–Q10: Identify the concern, state what you need to assess, recommend a safe action, and explain its physiological basis. Refer for clinical assessment when symptoms are concerning.
